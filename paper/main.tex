\begin{abstract}
Modern explicit graphics APIs make applications describe memory dependencies,
resource access scopes, and image layouts in detail. Portable APIs such as
WebGPU reconstruct and validate this state at run time, while render graphs
derive it from a declared workload. \blade{} explores a different point in the
design space: its Vulkan backend keeps ordinary images in
\texttt{GENERAL}, does not maintain per-resource states, and places a global
memory barrier at pass boundaries.

We measure that design against application-declared barrier placement inside
\blade{} and against matched \wgpu{} programs, on six GPUs from four vendors,
and we read the AMD open-source driver to establish what a coarse barrier
actually costs. Barrier \emph{placement} is the dimension that matters, but not
uniformly: removing redundant barriers between sixteen independent compute
passes is worth $\bmagci{rtx5070}{compute-independent}{placement}$ on an
RTX~5070 and $\bmagci{rx7900xt}{compute-independent}{placement}$ on an
RX~7900~XT, while the same change on independent \emph{render} passes is worth
$\bmag{rtx5070}{graphics-independent}{placement}$ on the NVIDIA part, at most
$\bmag{raphael}{graphics-independent}{placement}$ on either desktop AMD part,
and \emph{costs}
$\bmagci{radeon780m}{graphics-independent}{placement}$ on a Radeon~780M. When
passes genuinely form a chain, the redundant barrier is free everywhere. The
host side separates the two designs consistently: recording and submitting a
sixteen-pass command buffer costs \blade{}
$\bladenum{sweep/host/compute-independent/auto/at16}\,\mu$s against
$\bladenum{sweep/host/compute-independent/wgpu/at16}\,\mu$s for the matched
\wgpu{} program on the same device, with a marginal cost of
$\bladenum{sweep/host/compute-independent/auto/marginal}\,\mu$s versus
$\bladenum{sweep/host/compute-independent/wgpu/marginal}\,\mu$s per additional
pass.

Reading RADV shows what a coarse barrier asks for: a global
\texttt{MEMORY\_WRITE} dependency over \texttt{ALL\_COMMANDS} expands to every
write access, which forces a compute \emph{and} a pixel partial flush, flushes
the colour and depth blocks together with their metadata caches, and
invalidates L2. That reading suggested a further configuration, which we
implemented and measured: a barrier that is still global and still tracks no
resource, but declares stages and accesses derived from the kinds of the two
passes it separates. It is worth $\bmagci{rtx5070}{graphics-chain}{scope}$ of
GPU span on a dependent graphics chain on NVIDIA and
$\bmagci{intelxe}{graphics-chain}{scope}$ on Intel, where placement can recover
nothing at all --- and nothing measurable on AMD, which is where the driver
source predicted it would pay. We report that failed prediction as a result,
and then find the answer on the same page of the same driver: on the parts
measured, RADV already elides the destination-side cache invalidation that the
coarse barrier asks for. Counting the commands a barrier expands into bounds
what the driver is asked to do, not what it costs. Finally, the classic
argument against \texttt{GENERAL} has largely expired: RADV keeps DCC colour
compression in \texttt{GENERAL} on GFX10 and later, and keeps TC-compatible
HTILE, but disables FMASK compression, so multisampled colour is the surviving
exception.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Explicit GPU APIs expose synchronization so applications can preserve
parallelism and avoid unnecessary cache operations. That control has a cost:
the application or an abstraction layer must know how every buffer and image
subresource is used, maintain state across command buffers, validate conflicts,
and generate transitions. This machinery sits on a CPU-critical command
recording path. The \wgpu{} implementation describes its trackers as some of
the hottest code in its codebase~\cite{wgpu-trackers}.

\blade{}~\cite{blade} tests the opposite engineering hypothesis. Its public API has no
resource-state objects. On Vulkan, ordinary images remain in
\texttt{VK\_IMAGE\_LAYOUT\_GENERAL}; a broad memory barrier orders writes
before later reads and writes at pass boundaries. The design sacrifices
opportunities for overlap and resource-specific cache control in exchange for
a smaller API and negligible tracking work.

The question is newly relevant because
\texttt{VK\_KHR\_unified\_image\_layouts} guarantees, when enabled, that
\texttt{GENERAL} is as efficient as specialized layouts in most
uses~\cite{vulkan-unified-layouts}. The extension removes one reason for
tracking image state, but explicitly leaves barrier granularity as a separate
performance question. It is also not yet universal: of the five Vulkan
device-driver pairs in this study, four advertise it and one does not
(Table~\ref{tab:platforms}).

The immediate motivation is concrete. A \blade{} user reported that their
renderer's planar-reflection pass and main pass, which share no resources, were
being serialized on AMD hardware purely because \blade{} placed a barrier
between them~\cite{blade-issue-343}. That report, the fix it produced, and the
question of how far it generalizes are the subject of Section~\ref{sec:field}.

This paper is organized around the following research questions:

\begin{description}
  \item[RQ1:] How does host cost scale with passes and dependency density for
  tracking-free and tracked command recording?
  \item[RQ2:] When do global barriers reduce device overlap, and when are they
  free or even beneficial?
  \item[RQ3:] How do matched \blade{} and \wgpu{} implementations differ
  end-to-end?
  \item[RQ4:] How do the answers differ between compute and graphics workloads,
  and across architectures?
\end{description}

The contributions are:

\begin{itemize}
  \item a cost model that distinguishes tracking, barrier precision, barrier
  placement, and image layouts without claiming that every factor is
  independently controlled;
  \item a controlled within-\blade{} evaluation of automatic versus
  application-declared barrier placement on six GPUs, including a negative
  result where coarse placement wins;
  \item a barrier that narrows stage and access scope from the kinds of the
  surrounding passes, keeping the no-resource-state contract, with its
  correctness argument, its measured effect at fixed placement, and the
  failure of the driver-source reading that predicted where it would pay;
  \item matched \blade{} and \wgpu{} workloads with byte-identical outputs, for
  end-to-end comparison;
  \item a reading of the AMD open-source driver that quantifies, in driver
  operations rather than folklore, what a global barrier and a persistent
  \texttt{GENERAL} layout ask for --- and, from the same driver, the reason
  most of what they ask for is free on the parts we measured;
  \item practical guidance identifying the workload envelope in which
  tracking-free synchronization is appropriate.
\end{itemize}

\section{Background}

\subsection{Execution and memory dependencies}

A Vulkan barrier describes both an execution dependency between pipeline
stages and a memory dependency between access types. Resource-specific
barriers can additionally identify the affected buffer or image range.
Narrow scopes may preserve unrelated work, whereas overly broad scopes can
drain the pipeline~\cite{amd-barriers,nvidia-vulkan-tips}. A global memory
barrier is nevertheless attractive when many resources share the same
dependency because it combines them in one command and often maps to hardware
operations whose granularity is already coarse.

These dimensions must not be collapsed into a single ``barrier quality''
variable. A system may:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item place barriers only at true hazards or at every pass boundary;
  \item use global or resource-specific memory scopes;
  \item use broad or precise pipeline stages and access masks; and
  \item transition images between specialized layouts or retain one layout.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{What a coarse barrier costs a driver}
\label{sec:radv-barriers}

Vendor guidance states that broad barriers are expensive but rarely says what
the expense is. The AMD open-source Vulkan driver answers the question
directly, so we read it rather than infer it. All statements below refer to
Mesa \texttt{main} at revision \texttt{d18d598e275d}~\cite{mesa-radv}.

RADV converts a dependency into a set of cache-operation flags in
\texttt{radv\_src\_access\_flush} and \texttt{radv\_dst\_access\_flush}. Before
either runs, the shared Vulkan runtime expands the access
mask~\cite{mesa-vk-synchronization}: \texttt{VK\_ACCESS\_2\_MEMORY\_WRITE\_BIT}
becomes \emph{every} write access reachable from the given stage mask.
\blade's barrier names \texttt{ALL\_COMMANDS}, so the expansion is total, and
consequently every branch of \texttt{radv\_src\_access\_flush} fires:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{COLOR\_ATTACHMENT\_WRITE} triggers
  \texttt{FLUSH\_AND\_INV\_CB};
  \item \texttt{DEPTH\_STENCIL\_ATTACHMENT\_WRITE} triggers
  \texttt{FLUSH\_AND\_INV\_DB};
  \item \texttt{TRANSFER\_WRITE} triggers both again;
  \item \texttt{TRANSFORM\_FEEDBACK\_WRITE} adds a write-back of L2; and
  \item each of the others adds \texttt{INV\_L2} unless the driver can prove
  the image is L2-coherent.
\end{itemize}

Two details make the global form strictly worse than a resource-scoped form
carrying the same access masks. First, RADV initializes
\texttt{has\_CB\_meta} and \texttt{has\_DB\_meta} to true and only clears them
when an image argument is supplied; a global memory barrier therefore always
adds \texttt{FLUSH\_AND\_INV\_CB\_META} and \texttt{FLUSH\_AND\_INV\_DB\_META}
even when no compressed image is involved. The destination side is pessimistic
in the same way and for the same reason. Second, the L2-coherence check
\texttt{radv\_image\_is\_l2\_coherent} requires an image, so a global barrier
cannot use it; the source comment notes that for memory barriers without an
image the state at rest ``often devolves to just VRAM/GTT anyway''.

The execution dependency is separate and equally blunt. RADV maps source
stages to partial flushes: \texttt{ALL\_COMMANDS} appears in both the list that
sets \texttt{CS\_PARTIAL\_FLUSH} and the list that sets
\texttt{PS\_PARTIAL\_FLUSH}. A partial flush waits for all in-flight waves of
that type to retire. Placing \blade's barrier at every pass boundary therefore
drains both the compute and the pixel pipelines between every pair of passes,
whether or not they share data. This is a mechanism, not a conjecture, and it
matches the user report in Section~\ref{sec:field}.

\subsubsection{What the same driver takes back}
\label{sec:radv-elision}

The paragraphs above read the request. The driver's answer to it is a few lines
further down the same function, and we record it here because
Section~\ref{sec:scope-results} needed it and did not have it.

\texttt{radv\_dst\_access\_flush} does not use the barrier's masks alone. It
first widens its notion of coherence:
\texttt{image\_is\_coherent} is set whenever
\texttt{can\_skip\_buffer\_l2\_flushes} holds and no render-backend-incoherent
image is currently dirty. The first condition is a property of the part ---
GFX9, or GFX10 and later with a coherent TCC --- and the second is tracked
across the command buffer, set only when a render pass binds an attachment for
which \texttt{radv\_image\_is\_l2\_coherent} is false. On GFX10 and later that
predicate reduces to whether the image is pipe-misaligned, which ordinary
attachments are not.

The consequence is that on RDNA parts, for ordinary images, \emph{every
destination-side \texttt{INV\_L2} the coarse barrier asks for is dropped}. The
driver has already made the argument that motivated our narrower barrier, and
made it without needing the application to say anything. What a narrower scope
can still remove is the source-side flushes and the pixel drain --- and those
are the operations whose cost is proportional to work in flight and to dirty
cache state, both of which are absent in exactly the workloads where the
narrowing is cleanest to measure. Section~\ref{sec:scope-results} reports the
measurement; this is the explanation, arrived at afterwards, and it is a
reading of source rather than a second measurement.

\subsection{Image layouts}

Vulkan image layouts communicate usage information that may select compression
metadata, attachment paths, or transfer representations. Vendor guidance has
historically recommended specialized layouts, particularly on
AMD~\cite{amd-rdna-guide}. The unified-image-layouts extension reflects a
hardware and driver trend in which most layouts are physically identical. It
allows \texttt{GENERAL} in most positions and promises optimal performance,
while retaining exceptions such as initialization from
\texttt{UNDEFINED}, presentation, and some video interactions
~\cite{vulkan-unified-layouts}.

RADV lets us be more specific than the extension proposal, because the layout
policy is three readable predicates~\cite{mesa-radv}:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{radv\_layout\_dcc\_compressed} ends by returning true when the
  GFX level is at least GFX10 \emph{or} the layout is not \texttt{GENERAL}.
  Delta colour compression therefore survives \texttt{GENERAL} on RDNA and
  later, and is lost on GFX9 and earlier. One earlier branch qualifies this:
  an image whose queue-family mask includes the compute family loses DCC in
  \texttt{GENERAL} on hardware that cannot compress DCC through image stores.
  \blade{} creates every image \texttt{EXCLUSIVE} on one queue family, so the
  qualification does not apply to anything measured here, but it does apply to
  an application that shares images with an async-compute queue. Subject to
  that, the widely repeated advice to avoid \texttt{GENERAL} on AMD is accurate
  for pre-RDNA parts and obsolete for the parts most applications target today.
  \item \texttt{radv\_layout\_is\_htile\_compressed} returns true for
  \texttt{GENERAL} when TC-compatible HTILE is enabled for that level, the
  image can be used on the graphics queue, and a driver-configuration override
  has not disabled the case. The accompanying comment states that it exists
  specifically to help ``apps that use GENERAL for the main depth pass'', and
  gives ``the image doesn't have the storage bit set'' as part of its
  justification --- a condition the code does not itself test, because
  TC-compatible HTILE is only enabled for images a shader can read.
  \item \texttt{radv\_layout\_fmask\_compression} returns
  \texttt{RADV\_FMASK\_COMPRESSION\_NONE} for \texttt{GENERAL}
  unconditionally, before any of its other cases. Multisampled colour is the
  surviving case where a persistent \texttt{GENERAL} layout costs real
  bandwidth on AMD.
\end{itemize}

Consequently, this work uses ``no layout tracking'' to mean no steady-state
tracking for ordinary application images. It does not claim that all layout
transitions or image-specific synchronization disappear, and it identifies MSAA
colour as the case where the claim is weakest.

\subsection{WebGPU and \wgpu}

WebGPU defines usage scopes that reject conflicting uses and provide a safe,
portable programming contract~\cite{webgpu-spec}. \wgpu{} represents buffer
and texture states in dense trackers, merges per-scope usage, and generates
transitions between command buffers~\cite{wgpu-trackers}. Its Vulkan backend
emits \texttt{VkBufferMemoryBarrier} and \texttt{VkImageMemoryBarrier}
structures whose stages, accesses, and image layouts are derived from the
recorded usage, which is the ``precise'' configuration in every dimension. This
work does not treat that work as accidental overhead: it implements guarantees
that \blade's unsafe API intentionally does not provide.

The comparison instead asks what those guarantees and their precision cost,
and whether a narrower native contract is useful for applications that already
know their dependency structure.

\section{\blade{} Synchronization Model}

\blade{} exposes transfer, compute, and render passes within a command encoder.
It does not attach a current state to resource handles. The Vulkan backend
places the following dependency before each pass, and once more when the
encoder is finished:

\begin{verbatim}
srcStage  = ALL_COMMANDS
dstStage  = ALL_COMMANDS
srcAccess = MEMORY_WRITE
dstAccess = MEMORY_READ | MEMORY_WRITE
\end{verbatim}

Images are initialized from \texttt{UNDEFINED} to \texttt{GENERAL} and remain
in \texttt{GENERAL} for ordinary rendering, sampling, storage, and copies.
Presentation images still transition to the presentation layout. \blade{}
enables \texttt{VK\_KHR\_unified\_image\_layouts} on drivers that advertise it.

Since the resolution of the field report, \texttt{CommandEncoderDesc} carries a
\texttt{manual\_barriers} flag~\cite{blade-pr-355}. When set, the automatic pass-boundary barrier
is suppressed and the application calls \texttt{encoder.barrier()} where its
dependency graph requires one. The emitted barrier is identical; only the
placement changes. This is the mechanism the evaluation controls, and its
design history is Section~\ref{sec:field}.

\subsection{Narrowing the barrier without tracking resources}
\label{sec:scoped}

Section~\ref{sec:radv-barriers} shows that most of what the barrier above
\emph{asks} of RADV comes from the two words \texttt{ALL\_COMMANDS} and
\texttt{MEMORY\_WRITE}, not from the barrier being global. That suggests a
cheaper barrier that still tracks nothing: an encoder always knows what kind of
pass it just closed and what kind it is about to open, and a pass kind is one
enum per encoder, not a table indexed by resource. The distinction between what
a barrier asks for and what it costs is the one this configuration went on to
fail; we keep the original reasoning here because the configuration is
worth having anyway, and report the failure in
Section~\ref{sec:scope-results}.

We therefore added a second scope, and the first question it raises is how
much the application has to say for it to work. The answer is: nothing, for
the barriers the encoder places itself. Two quantities are needed, and the
encoder already holds both.

The \emph{source} scope must cover every write the barrier has to make
available. That is the union of the kinds of the passes recorded since the
previous barrier, which the encoder accumulates in one bitmask. Taking the
union rather than just the last pass is what makes this safe when the
application suppressed some boundaries: whichever passes went unseparated,
their writes are still in the set. The \emph{destination} scope is the kind of
the pass being opened, and the encoder is inside \texttt{compute()},
\texttt{render()}, \texttt{transfer()}, or
\texttt{acceleration\_structure()} when it places the barrier, so the kind is
the method that was called. Nothing is added to the pass declaration, and no
resource is consulted.

The one case that is not derivable is an explicitly placed barrier, because
the consumer has not been declared when \texttt{barrier()} is called. That one
takes its scope as an argument, and is emitted where it is written: its source
scope narrows like any other, but its destination stays \texttt{ALL\_COMMANDS}.
Holding the request back until the next pass opened would recover the
destination as well, and we measured that it does, but a synchronization
primitive that does not appear where the application put it is a poor trade for
a few percent. The asymmetry is instead reported as a result: it is the
measurable cost of not knowing what comes next.

The derivation itself is a table over pass kinds:

\begin{center}
\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{@{}l@{\ \ }l@{\ \ }l@{}}
\toprule
Pass kind & Source stage & Source access \\
\midrule
transfer & \texttt{TRANSFER} & \texttt{TRANSFER\_WRITE} \\
compute & \texttt{COMPUTE\_SHADER} & \texttt{SHADER\_WRITE} \\
render & \texttt{ALL\_GRAPHICS} & attachment, \texttt{SHADER\_WRITE} \\
accel.\ struct. & \texttt{AS\_BUILD} & \texttt{AS\_WRITE} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

The destination masks mirror this with the read accesses each kind can perform.
The first barrier of an encoder starts with the source set seeded to
``unknown'', so it is as wide as before; anything recorded outside the pass
model, such as image initialization, adds ``unknown'' to the set for the same
reason. The barrier emitted when the encoder finishes keeps a wide
\emph{destination}, since it has to reach the next submission and the host, but
its source is derived like any other.

Correctness rests on the standard availability-and-visibility argument: the
barrier that follows a pass performs the availability operation for that pass's
writes, and any later barrier whose destination scope covers a consumer makes
all previously available writes visible to it. Narrowing a source mask is
therefore safe as long as every write is made available by the next barrier
after it, which is what the accumulated source set guarantees.

Synchronization validation was necessary rather than decorative here. It found
that Blade applies two driver workarounds which add transfer accesses to every
barrier; those are legal alongside \texttt{ALL\_COMMANDS} and illegal alongside
a narrowed stage mask, and they are also unnecessary in the narrowed case,
because a narrowed scope names the accesses of the passes that actually ran.
The final implementation passes the Khronos synchronization checks with no
hazards and no validation errors on all thirty-six workload and policy
combinations, and every policy produces the same output hash.

On RADV the difference is concrete. A compute-to-compute boundary drops from
\{compute and pixel partial flush, colour-block flush, depth-block flush, both
metadata flushes, L2 invalidate, L2 write-back\} to \{compute partial flush, L2
invalidate\}. A render-to-compute boundary keeps the colour and depth flushes,
which are real, but loses the compute drain. What it does \emph{not} drop is
anything on the destination side, because on these parts the driver had already
dropped it (Section~\ref{sec:radv-elision}) --- which is the first hint that
this configuration would not pay where it was designed to.

The evaluated contract is deliberately limited:

\begin{itemize}
  \item synchronization within one Vulkan queue and command stream;
  \item explicit host and cross-queue synchronization outside the pass model;
  \item explicit initialization and presentation transitions; and
  \item native validation during correctness testing, but no safe-API
  guarantee in production.
\end{itemize}

The Metal backend is a different animal and should not be described as
tracking-free. Metal tracks hazards for resources created in the default
\texttt{MTLHazardTrackingModeTracked} mode~\cite{metal-hazard-tracking}, and
\blade{} relies on that: \texttt{manual\_barriers} has no effect there, and the
backend issues no inter-pass synchronization of its own. On Metal the tracking
has moved into the framework, not disappeared.

\section{Field Report: Barrier-Induced Serialization on AMD}
\label{sec:field}

The controlled experiments in this paper were prompted by a user
report~\cite{blade-issue-343}. A renderer built on \blade{} drew a planar
reflection and a main view into separate colour and depth targets, resolved
each from MSAA, and then drew reflective and refractive geometry that depended
on both. Conceptually the reflection and main passes are independent. In
practice \blade{} emitted this sequence:

\begin{verbatim}
full_barrier(); raytrace()
full_barrier(); reflection_depth_prepass()
pipeline_barrier(); render_reflection()
full_barrier(); resolve_reflection_msaa()
full_barrier(); main_depth_prepass()
pipeline_barrier(); render_main()
full_barrier(); resolve_main_msaa()
full_barrier(); render_reflective_refractive()
\end{verbatim}

The reporter observed in a GPU profiler that the barriers forced the AMD
scheduler to run the reflection work to completion before starting the main
view, and asked whether the hardware could overlap them at all if the barriers
were removed. Section~\ref{sec:radv-barriers} answers the mechanism question:
on RADV each \texttt{full\_barrier} sets both \texttt{CS\_PARTIAL\_FLUSH} and
\texttt{PS\_PARTIAL\_FLUSH}, so the drain is unconditional and does not depend
on the driver noticing that the two passes are unrelated. No amount of driver
cleverness recovers the overlap, because the application asked for a
dependency on \texttt{ALL\_COMMANDS} and the driver is entitled to believe it.

The reporter prototyped a flag to disable automatic barriers, removed the
barriers between the ray-tracing, reflection, and main passes, kept one before
the dependent refraction pass, and reported that the ray-tracing and reflection
passes then executed concurrently. Two API shapes were debated: a scoped
``group'' object whose passes share one barrier, and a mode flag on the
encoder. The group form was rejected as too much API surface for the
benefit, and per-call enable/disable was rejected because the scope of the
switch is ambiguous at the call site --- a concern the reporter later confirmed
by forgetting to re-enable it. The resolution~\cite{blade-pr-355} is a flag
fixed at encoder creation, which makes the mode a property of a whole encoder
and lets an application put its serial work in one encoder and its independent
work in another.

This paper's \texttt{B-hazard} configuration is exactly that mechanism applied
to a controlled workload, so the study can ask how far the field report
generalizes. Section~\ref{sec:placement} shows that it does not generalize
unconditionally, and Section~\ref{sec:discussion} proposes the boundary.

\subsection{Workloads}

The headless \texttt{sync-bench} artifact provides four workloads that vary one
factor: whether consecutive passes carry a true data dependency.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \texttt{compute-independent}: one shared read-only input buffer, a
  distinct output buffer per pass. No inter-pass hazard.
  \item \texttt{compute-chain}: two ping-pong storage buffers. Every pass
  depends on its predecessor.
  \item \texttt{graphics-independent}: a distinct \texttt{RGBA8} colour target
  per pass, cleared and stored. No inter-pass hazard.
  \item \texttt{graphics-chain}: one colour target loaded and stored with
  additive blending. Every pass depends on its predecessor.
  \item \texttt{mixed-independent} and \texttt{mixed-chain}: compute and render
  passes alternating, with the same resource patterns as the corresponding
  single-kind workloads applied within each family. Every pass boundary
  therefore joins two different pass kinds, which is where the scope axis has
  the most to give. In \texttt{mixed-chain} each pass depends on the pass of
  the same kind two positions earlier, so every boundary carries a real hazard
  and placement has nothing to remove.
\end{itemize}

The two mixed families exist only in \blade's benchmark; the matched \wgpu{}
program has no equivalent, and they are used for the within-\blade{}
comparison rather than the end-to-end one.

Unless stated otherwise, each command buffer contains 16 passes; compute
buffers hold $2^{20}$ \texttt{u32} elements, render targets are
$1024\times1024$, and each invocation performs 8 mixing rounds. Every
configuration is warmed for 10 iterations and measured for 30, three times, in
an order randomized jointly across implementations.

The matched \wgpu{} program uses the same WGSL, resource sizes, pass graph, and
iteration schedule, and supplies the 16-byte per-pass parameters through
\wgpu's native \texttt{IMMEDIATES} feature so that the \wgpu{} side does not
gain an extra tracked uniform buffer. \texttt{check-workload-equivalence.py}
verifies that the two shader pairs are textually identical modulo binding
decorations, and the collector aborts if the two implementations produce
different output hashes. All \bladenum{runs/matrix} runs of the fixed matrix
and all \bladenum{runs/sweep} runs of the two sweeps agree on the output hash
within each of the \bladenum{runs/hashgroups} workload and pass-count groups,
across every device, policy, and implementation, with
\bladenum{runs/hashconflicts} exceptions.

These six are the whole workload set, deliberately. They vary one factor ---
whether consecutive passes carry a dependency --- across the three pass kinds a
synchronization policy can distinguish, and that is what the study is about.
Application frames would add heterogeneity of a kind these do not have, and
Section~\ref{sec:threats} states what that costs the conclusions; they would
also add scene, content, and driver-heuristic variation that this design exists
to exclude. We report a bounded claim about a controlled workload rather than
an unbounded one about renderers.

\subsection{Metrics and statistics}

We measure command-buffer start, pass recording, queue submission, and host
wait separately, and report \emph{host cost} as recording plus submission,
because \wgpu{} defers command translation and tracker resolution to
\texttt{finish()} and \texttt{submit()} while \blade{} performs it during
recording. Charging only recording would misattribute the difference.

Device time is the GPU span of one command buffer. On Vulkan, \blade{} writes a
\texttt{TOP\_OF\_PIPE} timestamp before each pass and one after the final
barrier; consecutive differences telescope, so their sum is exactly the span
from the first pass to the end of the command buffer, and it remains
well-defined when passes overlap. The \wgpu{} program uses one
beginning-of-first-pass and one end-of-last-pass timestamp. \blade's window is
therefore the wider of the two, which is conservative against \blade.

We report medians, quartiles, and 95\% bootstrap intervals with a
deterministic per-configuration seed. Percent differences are relative to the
\texttt{B-auto} median of the same device and workload. Validation layers are
enabled for correctness runs and disabled for performance runs, as recommended
by vendor guidance~\cite{nvidia-vulkan-tips}.

We did not preregister a practical-equivalence region, and in the end we do not
use one. An earlier version of this analysis derived a single $\pm3\%$ band
from the worst \texttt{B-explicit-all} control deviation; with all
\bladenum{floor/count} cells collected, control deviations run from $0.0\%$ to
$\bladenum{floor/max}\%$, so no single band describes them. Every claim is
therefore compared against the control floor of the cell it is made in, which
Table~\ref{tab:scope} reports alongside it. A band wide enough to cover the
worst cell would discard the best ones, and a band fitted to the best would
license the worst.

\subsection{Deviations from the protocol}
\label{sec:deviations}

Five deviations affect how the results may be read, and are stated here rather
than in a footnote.

\textbf{Device mismatch on \texttt{rubik}, since repaired.} That machine has
both a Radeon~RX~7900~XT and the small Raphael integrated GPU. In the first
collection \blade{} selected the integrated device and \wgpu{} the discrete
one, so its cross-implementation cells compared different hardware. The
collector now aborts when the two implementations disagree about the device,
and it collects every enumerated adapter in turn rather than one; \texttt{rubik}
has been recollected as two separate collections, one per GPU, with both
implementations pinned to the same device in each. The defective collection is
not retained --- it is superseded by correct data for the same hardware --- but
it is the reason the check exists.

\textbf{Metal timestamps.} \blade's Metal backend reports per-pass durations
from counter sample buffers rather than a span, so summing them double-counts
overlapping passes; the matched \wgpu{} program returned zero for three of the
four workloads. Metal device time is therefore not reported at all, and
Section~\ref{sec:metal} uses host wall time. The machine also had macOS Low
Power Mode enabled, which is recorded in the collection but not controlled.

\textbf{Clocks that move during a measurement.} Table~\ref{tab:scope} carries a
control floor for every cell: the disagreement between two configurations that
emit identical commands. Of the \bladenum{floor/count} cells,
\bladenum{floor/above2} have floors above $2\%$ and \bladenum{floor/above30}
above $30\%$; the largest of the rest is $\bladenum{floor/below2max}\%$. Any
effect smaller than its cell's floor is not a result and is not treated as one.

The cause is visible in the time-ordered samples, and it is not what we first
wrote down. Each block --- one workload, one policy, one repetition --- is a
separate process launch of thirty measured iterations after ten warm-ups, so
the device idles between blocks and ramps its clock again inside each one. On
the RX~7900~XT a \texttt{compute-chain} block begins at $245\,\mu$s, holds for
fourteen iterations, then steps down through $225$, $217$, $205$ to
$193\,\mu$s at the last sample, and is still descending when measurement
stops. The block never reaches steady state, so its median is a function of
where the steps fell rather than of the configuration under test.

Comparing the median of a block's first third with its last third makes this a
number. It separates the machines exactly as the control floors do: the
RTX~5070 drifts $\bladenum{drift/rtx5070/median}\%$ at the median block and the
Raphael iGPU $\bladenum{drift/raphael/median}\%$, and both have floors at or
below $\bfloor{rtx5070}{graphics-chain}$ in their best cells. The RX~7900~XT
drifts $\bladenum{drift/rx7900xt/median}\%$, the Radeon~780M
$\bladenum{drift/radeon780m/median}\%$, and the Intel part
$\bladenum{drift/intelxe/median}\%$ at the median block but
$\bladenum{drift/intelxe/worst}\%$ in its worst --- a single step of that size
partway through a block, which is what makes its pooled samples look bimodal
and its floors reach $\bladenum{floor/max}\%$. Whether a given block ramps
depends on how long the device idled before it, which is why two blocks of the
same cell can disagree so much more than the samples within either.

This matters for what to do about it. The RX~7900~XT collection was taken with
\texttt{power\_dpm\_force\_performance\_level} set to \texttt{high} on both of
its devices, recorded in the collection, and it drifted anyway: the setting
raises the ceiling and does not stop the ramp, and the driver still reports a
$35\,$MHz shader clock at idle underneath it. Pinning clocks is therefore not
the fix we assumed it was. Warming up until the device stops moving is, and it
is a parameter rather than a change: the ramp here needs more than the forty
iterations a block contains. Both are cheap, and a repetition of this study
should do both and check the drift before believing the floor.

\textbf{Host measurements on \texttt{\bladenum{host/excluded/machines}}.} These
are reported in Table~\ref{tab:host-matrix} and used for nothing. At the median
16-pass block that machine's host samples spread
$\bladenum{host/excluded/dispersion}\%$ of their own median, against at most
$\bladenum{host/usable/dispersion}\%$ on every other machine, while its
\emph{device} blocks are as clean as anyone's. That is CPU-side interference,
and it is confined to individual blocks --- which is exactly what a control
floor cannot see, since both control configurations sit inside the same block.
Its effect on a summary is not subtle: including that machine, the
\wgpu{}-to-\blade{} host ratio ranges from $\bladenum{host/ratio/all/min}\times$
to $\bladenum{host/ratio/all/max}\times$ and both ends come from it; excluding
it, the remaining \bladenum{host/ratio/count} cells range from
$\bladenum{host/ratio/min}\times$ to $\bladenum{host/ratio/max}\times$. We
report the second and show the first.

\textbf{Collections span several commits.} The machines were measured over two
days and each ran the revision listed in Table~\ref{tab:platforms}. The
measured code --- \texttt{blade-graphics} and the benchmark --- is identical
across the Vulkan revisions apart from the addition of a
\texttt{--capture} flag that does nothing unless passed; the Metal collection
predates the scope axis, which is why it contributes no scoped configurations.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Control validity}

The \texttt{B-explicit} column of Table~\ref{tab:gpu-matrix} is the
instrumentation control: manual barriers before every pass reproduce the
automatic policy's command stream exactly, so any difference is measurement
noise. Its value in a given cell is the floor below which that cell
cannot answer anything, and it is reported as the \texttt{ctrl} column of
Table~\ref{tab:scope}.

Two choices about that column matter more than they look. It is read per cell,
because floors range from $\bfloor{rx7900xt}{compute-independent}$ to
$\bfloor{rx7900xt}{compute-chain}$ within one device and from
$\bfloor{intelxe}{graphics-chain}$ to $\bfloor{intelxe}{mixed-independent}$
within another, so no device is uniformly trustworthy or uniformly useless. And
it is the far end of the control's interval rather than its point estimate: two
configurations emitting identical commands can agree closely on the median and
still disagree by $70\%$ across resamples, which is a statement about the cell
and not about the configurations. A control that only ever confirmed the clean
cells would not have been worth running; this one disqualifies
\bladenum{floor/above2} of \bladenum{floor/count} cells and licenses the rest.

It also earns its keep as a diagnostic rather than only as a gate.
Section~\ref{sec:deviations} identifies what the disqualified cells have in
common, and the answer --- a device still accelerating while it is being
measured --- was found by asking what the control was reacting to, not by
suspecting it in advance.

\subsection{Barrier placement and dependency structure}
\label{sec:placement}

\input{data/derived/tables/gpu-matrix}

Table~\ref{tab:gpu-matrix} answers RQ2 and RQ4 together. The pattern is not
``coarse barriers are slow''; it is conditional in a way that is stable across
devices for the dependent workloads and unstable for the independent ones.

\textbf{When the barrier is needed, it is free.} For the chain workloads,
\texttt{B-hazard} places a barrier before every pass except the first, so it
differs from \texttt{B-auto} by exactly one barrier out of sixteen. Across the five Vulkan devices, only
\bladenum{chain/resolved} of the fifteen chain cells resolve any difference at
all above their own control floor, and the larger of those is
$\bladenum{chain/maxresolved}\%$ --- against effects of up to
$\bmag{rtx5070}{graphics-independent}{placement}$ in the independent cells of
the same devices. Applications whose passes genuinely form a chain pay
approximately nothing for \blade's policy.

\textbf{When the barrier is redundant, the cost depends on the device and on
the kind of work.} For independent \emph{compute} passes, removing the
redundant barriers is worth $\bmagci{rtx5070}{compute-independent}{placement}$
on the RTX~5070 and $\bmagci{rx7900xt}{compute-independent}{placement}$ on the
RX~7900~XT: both large discrete GPUs have idle capacity that a redundant
barrier prevents them from using. The AMD figure is the one that changed most
between collections, and its control floor,
$\bfloor{rx7900xt}{compute-independent}$, is a quarter of the effect.

The Intel part reads $\bpct{intelxe}{compute-independent}{placement}$ in the
same cell and we do not claim it. Its control floor there is
$\bfloor{intelxe}{compute-independent}$, because that machine's
independent-target blocks step down by nearly half partway through the
measurement as the device finishes accelerating, so a block's median records
when the step happened. All \bladenum{floor/above30} cells with floors above
$30\%$ are on that machine, and nothing is read from them in either direction,
including their large apparent effects.

For independent \emph{render} passes the same change is worth
$\bmagci{rtx5070}{graphics-independent}{placement}$ on the RTX~5070 and
essentially nothing on either desktop AMD device ---
$\bpctci{rx7900xt}{graphics-independent}{placement}$ on the RX~7900~XT, which
its floor of $\bfloor{rx7900xt}{graphics-independent}$ cannot separate from
zero, and $\bpctci{raphael}{graphics-independent}{placement}$ on the Raphael
iGPU, which is resolved and is still nothing. This is the sharpest vendor split
in the study, and it means the headline ``placement dominates'' is carried by
one vendor for graphics and by two for compute.

\textbf{Removing barriers can make things worse.} On the Radeon~780M,
\texttt{B-hazard} \emph{costs} $\bmagci{radeon780m}{graphics-independent}{placement}$
on \texttt{graphics-independent} and
$\bmagci{radeon780m}{mixed-independent}{placement}$ on
\texttt{mixed-independent}, against control floors of
$\bfloor{radeon780m}{graphics-independent}$ and
$\bfloor{radeon780m}{mixed-independent}$. This is a fresh collection on the
current revision and it reproduces a regression first seen on an earlier one,
so it is not an artifact of either.

The obvious mechanism is memory-system pressure: sixteen concurrent
$1024\times1024$ \texttt{RGBA8} targets are 64\,MB of write traffic with no
reuse, and an integrated GPU shares its bandwidth and last-level cache with the
CPU, so serializing the passes keeps one target resident at a time. One cell in
the same row argues against it. The matched \wgpu{} program emits the same
single barrier as \texttt{B-hazard} on this workload
(Section~\ref{sec:generated}), so it lets the same sixteen passes overlap --- and
it is $\bmag{radeon780m}{graphics-independent}{wgpu}$ \emph{faster} than
\texttt{B-auto} rather than
$\bmag{radeon780m}{graphics-independent}{placement}$ slower. Whatever the
780M is punishing, it is not overlap as such, or the tracked implementation
would be punished for it too. We do not know what it is. Distinguishing the
candidates --- bandwidth, the \texttt{GENERAL} layout, power sharing with the
CPU --- needs counters this study did not collect, and we would rather leave
the mechanism open than pick the tidy one.

\textbf{A saturated device has nothing to gain.} On the Raphael iGPU every
\blade{} configuration is within $2\%$ of every other and every floor is at or
below $\bfloor{raphael}{compute-chain}$, so the intervals mean it:
two compute units are fully occupied by a single pass, so there is no idle
capacity for a second pass to fill regardless of what the barriers say.

This is the boundary the field report does not reveal on its own. Overlap pays
when adjacent passes have complementary bottlenecks and the device has idle
capacity --- ray tracing next to rasterization, in the reported case. It does not
pay when each pass already saturates the same resource, and it can lose when
running them together exceeds what the memory system can absorb.

\subsection{Barrier scope at fixed placement}
\label{sec:scope-results}

\input{data/derived/tables/scope}

Table~\ref{tab:scope} isolates the axis introduced in
Section~\ref{sec:scoped}. The scoped policies emit the same number of barriers
at the same boundaries as their unscoped twins; only the declared stages and
accesses change. The two chain workloads make the separation sharp, because
there placement has nothing to remove --- every boundary is a real hazard ---
so any difference must come from scope.

The result contradicts the prediction that motivated the configuration, and
that is the most useful thing about it.

\textbf{Where it pays.} On the RTX~5070 narrowing the scope is worth
$\bmagci{rtx5070}{graphics-chain}{scope}$ on \texttt{graphics-chain} and
$\bmagci{rtx5070}{mixed-chain}{scope}$ on \texttt{mixed-chain}, against control
floors of $\bfloor{rtx5070}{graphics-chain}$ and $\bfloor{rtx5070}{mixed-chain}$
in those cells. It is not confined to chains on that device: the same change is
worth $\bmagci{rtx5070}{graphics-independent}{scope}$ on
\texttt{graphics-independent}, floor $\bfloor{rtx5070}{graphics-independent}$,
where it is available \emph{in addition} to the much larger placement effect.
On the Intel~Xe part it is worth $\bmagci{intelxe}{graphics-chain}{scope}$ on
\texttt{graphics-chain}, floor $\bfloor{intelxe}{graphics-chain}$, and
$\bmagci{intelxe}{compute-chain}{scope}$ on \texttt{compute-chain}, floor
$\bfloor{intelxe}{compute-chain}$ --- both of them cells where barrier
placement can recover nothing at all, and the second of them only just clear of
its floor.

\textbf{Where it does not.} On the Raphael iGPU, whose floors are
$\bfloor{raphael}{compute-chain}$ or better in every cell, no scope effect
exceeds $\bmag{raphael}{compute-chain}{scope}$: that is a measured zero, not a
failure to measure. On the Radeon~780M the two compute cells are the clean
ones, with floors of $\bfloor{radeon780m}{compute-independent}$ and
$\bfloor{radeon780m}{compute-chain}$, and the scope effect in both is
$\bmag{radeon780m}{compute-independent}{scope}$ --- another measured zero, on a
different AMD generation. The RX~7900~XT is the machine that answers least: its
floors on this axis run from $\bfloor{rx7900xt}{graphics-chain}$ to
$\bfloor{rx7900xt}{mixed-chain}$ and no scope effect on it clears its own cell,
so it neither supports nor contradicts the other two. Every AMD cell precise
enough to answer says nothing happened.

This is the reverse of the prediction. Section~\ref{sec:radv-barriers} derives
from RADV that narrowing the scope sheds a pixel-pipeline drain, two attachment
flushes, and two metadata flushes per boundary, and it is right about the
commands --- that part is read from the source. It is wrong about the money.
The saving shows up on the two drivers whose source we did not read and not on
the one we did.

Having been wrong once from the source, we went back to it, and the answer was
there (Section~\ref{sec:radv-elision}): on GFX10 and later with a coherent TCC,
\texttt{radv\_dst\_access\_flush} drops every destination-side L2 invalidation
unless a render-backend-incoherent attachment is dirty, which for ordinary
images it never is. Half of what our narrower barrier was designed to save was
already being discarded by the driver, without the application saying anything.
What remains for the narrowing to remove is the source-side flushes and the
pixel drain --- and a partial flush costs time only if there is work in flight
to drain, while a metadata flush costs time only if the metadata caches are
dirty. On a dependent chain neither holds.

The general mistake is worth naming, because it is easy to repeat and we made
it in the obvious way. Counting the commands a barrier expands into measures
how much the driver is being \emph{asked} to do, which is an upper bound on
what it will \emph{cost} and can be an arbitrarily loose one. We read the half
of the driver that translates a request and not the half that decides what to
do about it. Reading it was still worth doing --- it produced the
configuration, and it now explains the result --- but the explanation arrived
after the measurement, and would not have been trusted before it.

Section~\ref{sec:generated} closes the argument with captures: the command
streams submitted on all \bladenum{captures/machines} machines are
byte-identical, so the spread from $\bmag{rtx5070}{graphics-chain}{scope}$ to
nothing is entirely in how each driver answers, not in what it was asked.

\textbf{Noise is a property of the cell, not the device.} The \texttt{ctrl}
column makes this legible, and it does not respect reputations. The Intel part
is the clearest case: its \texttt{graphics-chain} floor is
$\bfloor{intelxe}{graphics-chain}$, tight enough to resolve a $3\%$ effect,
while its \texttt{mixed-independent} floor is
$\bfloor{intelxe}{mixed-independent}$ and answers nothing --- the same device,
the same session, the same afternoon. The RX~7900~XT splits the other way,
with its best cell at $\bfloor{rx7900xt}{compute-independent}$ and its worst at
$\bfloor{rx7900xt}{mixed-chain}$. Two earlier versions of this analysis got
this wrong in opposite directions: one took the worst cell per device as that
device's floor, which discarded every usable AMD and Intel cell; the other took
the control's point estimate rather than its interval, which licensed a
$14\%$ reading from a cell that moves by nearly half while it is being read.

\textbf{Manual placement forfeits the scope saving.} The third column is not
the sum of the first two. Combining hazard-only placement with the narrow
scope reproduces the placement effect and nothing more: on the RTX~5070,
$\bpct{rtx5070}{graphics-chain}{both}$ on \texttt{graphics-chain} where scope
alone was worth $\bpct{rtx5070}{graphics-chain}{scope}$, and
$\bpct{rtx5070}{mixed-chain}{both}$ on \texttt{mixed-chain} where scope alone
was worth $\bpct{rtx5070}{mixed-chain}{scope}$. The reason is the asymmetry of
Section~\ref{sec:scoped}: under hazard-only placement every surviving barrier
is one the application wrote, so it narrows its source but not its destination,
and on these workloads the destination carried the saving.

That is the opposite of the intuition that more control is strictly better. An
application that leaves placement to the encoder gets a barrier the encoder
can describe on both sides; one that takes placement into its own hands gets a
barrier that only half describes itself. It is also the cheaper of the two
gains to obtain --- no API change and no resource tracking --- so where it
exists it should be taken first, and where placement is also available the two
should not be assumed to compose.

\subsection{Scaling with pass count}

\input{data/derived/tables/sweep}

Table~\ref{tab:sweep} sweeps the pass count on the RTX~5070 and answers RQ1.
Both cost components are linear in the number of passes over this range, so
each configuration is summarized by a marginal cost per pass.

On the host side, a redundant global barrier costs \blade{}
$\bladenum{sweep/host/compute-independent/barriercost}\,\mu$s per pass for
compute ($\bladenum{sweep/host/compute-independent/auto/marginal}$ against
$\bladenum{sweep/host/compute-independent/hazard/marginal}$) and
$\bladenum{sweep/host/graphics-independent/barriercost}\,\mu$s for graphics
($\bladenum{sweep/host/graphics-independent/auto/marginal}$ against
$\bladenum{sweep/host/graphics-independent/hazard/marginal}$). The matched
\wgpu{} program costs
$\bladenum{sweep/host/compute-independent/wgpu/marginal}$ and
$\bladenum{sweep/host/graphics-independent/wgpu/marginal}\,\mu$s per pass,
which is $\bladenum{sweep/host/compute-independent/wgpuoverauto}\times$ and
$\bladenum{sweep/host/graphics-independent/wgpuoverauto}\times$ \blade's
automatic policy and
$\bladenum{sweep/host/compute-independent/wgpuoverhazard}\times$ and
$\bladenum{sweep/host/graphics-independent/wgpuoverhazard}\times$ \blade{} with
no barrier at all. At sixteen passes the absolute gap is
$\bladenum{sweep/host/compute-independent/auto/at16}$ against
$\bladenum{sweep/host/compute-independent/wgpu/at16}\,\mu$s.

On the device side, each redundant barrier costs
$\bladenum{sweep/gpu/compute-independent/barriercost}\,\mu$s of GPU span for
compute ($\bladenum{sweep/gpu/compute-independent/auto/marginal}$ against
$\bladenum{sweep/gpu/compute-independent/hazard/marginal}$ per pass) and
$\bladenum{sweep/gpu/graphics-independent/barriercost}\,\mu$s for graphics
($\bladenum{sweep/gpu/graphics-independent/auto/marginal}$ against
$\bladenum{sweep/gpu/graphics-independent/hazard/marginal}$). Against a pass
that performs roughly $11\,\mu$s and $9\,\mu$s of work respectively, that is a
quarter of a pass lost per boundary. The ratio is what makes the effect visible
at any pass count: it is a property of the pass, not of the graph size.

The sweep is also why host claims are taken from the timestamp-free
collection. The same measurement is larger with GPU timing enabled, on both
implementations, by a margin that grows with the pass count. Neither
implementation is being measured unfairly --- the effect is present in both ---
but the absolute host numbers in a GPU-timed collection are not the recording
cost of the workload, which is why Table~\ref{tab:host-matrix} is used for
direction and Table~\ref{tab:sweep} for magnitude.

\subsection{Host cost across devices}

\input{data/derived/tables/host-matrix}

Table~\ref{tab:host-matrix} repeats the host comparison on every machine. Over
the \bladenum{host/ratio/count} cells whose host measurements this collection
resolves, the matched \wgpu{} program costs between
$\bladenum{host/ratio/min}\times$ and $\bladenum{host/ratio/max}\times$ more
host time than \blade's automatic policy for the same sixteen passes, and the

\texttt{\bladenum{host/excluded/machines}} row is shown but not used, for the

\subsection{Where the host time goes}
\label{sec:profile}

\input{data/derived/tables/profile}

Table~\ref{tab:profile} attributes each implementation's CPU time to the crate
or library that spent it, on a workload deliberately shaped so the process
stays in the recording path rather than blocking on the GPU. Two machines are
profiled, one NVIDIA and one RADV, and they disagree by enough to be worth
showing side by side.

The result is a correction to the obvious reading of
Table~\ref{tab:host-matrix}. Resource state tracking is
$\bladenum{profile/zork/wgpu/compute-independent/wgputracker}\%$ of \wgpu's
process CPU time on \texttt{compute-independent} on \texttt{zork} and
$\bladenum{profile/rubik/wgpu/compute-independent/wgputracker}\%$ on
\texttt{rubik} --- real, and on both machines the largest single \wgpu{}
component after command recording, but nowhere near enough to explain a gap of
several times. Across both machines and both workloads the tracker runs
$\bladenum{profile/wgpu/tracker/min}$--$\bladenum{profile/wgpu/tracker/max}\%$
and every \wgpu{} row together
$\bladenum{profile/wgpu/wgputotal/min}$--$\bladenum{profile/wgpu/wgputotal/max}\%$.
Even if all of \wgpu{} were free above the driver, the remaining process would
still be more expensive than \blade's.

The factor of three between the two machines is itself informative: the
tracker's share is a share of a total that the driver dominates, and the two
drivers charge very differently for the same submissions. It is not evidence
that \wgpu's tracker is slower on AMD.

What actually dominates both implementations is the kernel and the driver:
$\bladenum{profile/blade/system/min}$--$\bladenum{profile/blade/system/max}\%$
of \blade's profile and
$\bladenum{profile/wgpu/system/min}$--$\bladenum{profile/wgpu/system/max}\%$
of \wgpu's, in every column. These workloads submit a command buffer per iteration with very
little GPU work in it, so submission cost is the bulk of the process. That also
means the two totals are not comparable --- \texttt{task-clock} charges a
blocking fence wait to the process whenever the driver spins inside it, and
\blade{} and \wgpu{} wait differently --- which is why the table reports shares
within a process rather than times between processes.

Four limits on this evidence. Repeating the profile of one configuration moves
its shares by around a percentage point, so these are quoted to the nearest
whole percent and small differences between rows are not meaningful. Self time
is charged to the symbol a sample landed in, so tracker work that the optimizer
inlined into its callers is charged to them; the tracker row is a lower bound.
Unlike the timing collections, the profiling step does not pin an adapter, so
on a multi-GPU machine the two implementations may not have profiled the same
device; only within-process shares are read from the table for that reason. And
the profile explains where the time goes, not why one design needs more of it:
the answer to that is spread across \wgpu's command recording, allocation, and
driver interaction rather than concentrated anywhere this table can point at.

\subsection{End-to-end \blade{} versus \wgpu}

The \texttt{W-wgpu} column of Table~\ref{tab:gpu-matrix} is more interesting
than a single ratio suggests, because its sign changes.

On the RTX~5070, \wgpu{} is $\bmag{rtx5070}{graphics-independent}{wgpu}$
\emph{faster} than \texttt{B-auto} on \texttt{graphics-independent} and
$\bmag{rtx5070}{compute-independent}{wgpu}$--$\bmag{rtx5070}{graphics-chain}{wgpu}$
slower everywhere else. The graphics-independent cell is what precise tracking
is supposed to buy: \wgpu{} knows the sixteen render targets are distinct and
emits no dependency between the passes, so the hardware overlaps them. It is
also a bounded result --- \texttt{B-hazard} on the same device is
$\bmag{rtx5070}{graphics-independent}{placement}$ faster than \texttt{B-auto},
so an application that declares its own graph beats the tracker by a wide
margin on the same hardware. Precise tracking recovers part of the available
overlap; explicit knowledge recovers more.

On the AMD parts the sign flips. \wgpu{} is faster than \texttt{B-auto} on
three of the four shared workloads on the RX~7900~XT ---
$\bmag{rx7900xt}{compute-independent}{wgpu}$ on \texttt{compute-independent},
$\bmag{rx7900xt}{graphics-chain}{wgpu}$ on \texttt{graphics-chain},
$\bmag{rx7900xt}{compute-chain}{wgpu}$ on \texttt{compute-chain} --- and faster
on both graphics workloads on the Raphael iGPU while being
$\bmag{raphael}{compute-independent}{wgpu}$--$\bmag{raphael}{compute-chain}{wgpu}$
slower on both compute ones. The largest of these reproduces on two independent
collections and is far outside its cell's control floor. So the tracked
implementation is not uniformly paying for its precision on the device side; on
this AMD part it is collecting on it.

We cannot explain any of these, in either direction, and the captures rule out
the explanation we would have reached for first. On the Intel device \wgpu's
GPU span is
$\bmag{intelxe}{graphics-independent}{wgpu}$--$\bmag{intelxe}{graphics-chain}{wgpu}$
\emph{larger} than \texttt{B-auto}'s, and since \texttt{compute-chain} is fully
serialized in both implementations that gap cannot be an overlap difference. It
is not a barrier-count difference either: Section~\ref{sec:generated} counts
the two implementations emitting the same number of barriers at the same
boundaries on the chains. What is left is the memory scope of each barrier, the
layout the images sit in, or something outside synchronization entirely, such
as descriptor or dispatch setup. Separating those would need a configuration
this study does not have --- \blade{} emitting resource-scoped barriers, which
is the tracking it exists to avoid --- so we leave it open rather than
attribute it.

\subsection{Apple/Metal case study}
\label{sec:metal}

\input{data/derived/tables/metal}

Metal is not pooled into the Vulkan comparison, because it hides image layouts
and because the framework performs the hazard tracking that \blade{} declines
to perform on Vulkan~\cite{metal-hazard-tracking}. On Metal, \blade{} is not a
tracking-free implementation; it is a client of Metal's tracker.

Table~\ref{tab:metal} therefore reports only what is comparable. \blade's host
cost is $\bladenum{metal/hostshare/min}$--$\bladenum{metal/hostshare/max}\%$ of
\wgpu's, and its wall time waiting for completion is
$\bladenum{metal/waitsaved/min}$--$\bladenum{metal/waitsaved/max}\%$ lower.
Note that \blade's Metal host cost
($\bladenum{metal/host/min}$--$\bladenum{metal/host/max}\,\mu$s for sixteen
passes) is about $\bladenum{metal/overvulkan/max}$ times its Vulkan host cost
on \texttt{zork}, because each \blade{} pass creates a new \texttt{MTL} command
encoder; the Metal backend has room to improve independently of anything in
this paper.

The question Metal actually poses --- what hazard tracking costs when the driver
rather than the application performs it --- is not answered by these workloads,
because both implementations pay it. A separate harness,
\texttt{paper/tools/metal-hazard-bench.swift}, answers it directly by creating
the same resources with \texttt{MTLHazardTrackingModeTracked} and
\texttt{MTLHazardTrackingModeUntracked} and, in the untracked dependent case,
reconstructing the dependency with an \texttt{MTLFence} or an
\texttt{MTLEvent}. Each pass is one compute encoder with one single-thread
dispatch, so the measurement is dominated by synchronization rather than by
work. The full protocol and tables are in the repository's investigation
note~\cite{blade-metal-note}.

The result inverts the Vulkan conclusion. For \emph{independent} passes,
turning tracking off changes nothing: encode time differs by $-0.9\%$ to
$+1.0\%$ and GPU time by $-0.4\%$ to $0.0\%$ at 1, 10, 100, and 500 passes.
Metal's tracker already knows the resources are distinct, so there is no
serialization to remove --- the exact condition that made
\texttt{manual\_barriers} valuable on Vulkan is absent. For \emph{dependent}
passes, opting out is expensive: at 100 passes an \texttt{MTLFence} costs
$8.9\times$ the encode time and $10.1\times$ the GPU time of the tracker, and
an \texttt{MTLEvent} costs $8.5\times$ and $10.3\times$; at 500 passes the
ratios are $8.7\times$/$11.1\times$ and $8.4\times$/$11.2\times$.

Two caveats apply. The machine was on battery with Low Power Mode enabled, so
the absolute times are not peak M3 numbers; the interleaved
tracked/untracked comparison is unaffected, but absolute claims should be
repeated on AC power. And the harness's deliberately unsynchronized untracked
case happened to produce correct values here, which is not evidence that it is
safe.

The design consequence for \blade{} is that \texttt{manual\_barriers} should
stay a Vulkan concept. Metal's tracking mode is chosen per resource at
creation, while \blade's flag belongs to a command encoder, and a resource
outlives any one encoder; moreover \blade's \texttt{barrier()} is called
between RAII pass encoders, after the producer's encoder has already ended,
which is too late to place the producer-side \texttt{updateFence}. The cost
model follows the abstraction: \blade's Vulkan fallback is coarse enough that
removing barriers exposes overlap, whereas Metal's tracker is precise enough
that opting out only forces the application to rebuild what the driver already
knew.

\subsection{Generated barriers and image layouts}
\label{sec:generated}

RenderDoc captures of all six workloads under three policies confirm what
Sections~\ref{sec:radv-barriers} and~\ref{sec:scoped} describe from source. The
counts are exactly the contract: with four passes recorded, \texttt{B-auto}
emits \bladenum{barriers/auto/independent} barriers per configuration --- one
per boundary plus the one the encoder emits on finish ---
\texttt{B-hazard} emits \bladenum{barriers/hazard/independent} on the
independent workloads and \bladenum{barriers/hazard/chain} on the chains, and
the scoped policies emit the same number as their unscoped twins.

The masks match too. Every \texttt{B-auto} barrier in every workload is the
same shape, \texttt{ALL\_COMMANDS} to \texttt{ALL\_COMMANDS}, with
\texttt{TRANSFER\_WRITE\,|\,MEMORY\_WRITE} made available to the four reads and
writes \texttt{TRANSFER\_READ}, \texttt{TRANSFER\_WRITE},
\texttt{MEMORY\_READ}, and \texttt{MEMORY\_WRITE} ---
including the two driver-workaround accesses. Under \texttt{B-scoped} the
derivation is visible in the capture: compute-to-compute boundaries carry
\texttt{COMPUTE\_SHADER} to \texttt{DRAW\_INDIRECT\,|\,COMPUTE\_SHADER},
render-to-render carry \texttt{ALL\_GRAPHICS} on both sides, mixed workloads
show both crossings, the first barrier of each encoder still carries
\texttt{ALL\_COMMANDS} on the source side, the barrier emitted on finish still
carries it on the destination side, and the workaround accesses are absent, as
intended.

Two things the capture settles that source reading could not. Every barrier in
the steady state is a global memory barrier: no buffer or image barrier appears
in any configuration. And no image layout transition appears at all --- images
are moved to \texttt{GENERAL} at initialization and stay there, which is the
layout claim the paper makes, observed rather than asserted.

The matched \wgpu{} program was captured the same way, which settles two
factors this study could previously only argue about.

\emph{Placement.} \wgpu{} emits exactly as many barriers as
\texttt{B-hazard}: \bladenum{barriers/wgpu/independent} on the independent
workloads, \bladenum{barriers/wgpu/chain} on the chains, where \texttt{B-auto}
emits \bladenum{barriers/auto/independent}. The tracker derives precisely the
placement the application declares by hand. Section~\ref{sec:placement}
inferred this from GPU spans; it is now counted.

\emph{Memory scope.} Every \blade{} barrier is a global memory barrier and
every \wgpu{} barrier is resource-scoped --- a \texttt{VkBufferMemoryBarrier}
for the compute workloads, a \texttt{VkImageMemoryBarrier} for the graphics
ones. Neither implementation emits a barrier of the other's kind in any
configuration. This is factor~2, which the experiment contract lists as
observed-only, and it is now observed rather than read from source.

\emph{Layouts.} The most useful thing the captures say is negative. \wgpu's
image barriers carry \texttt{COLOR\_ATTACHMENT\_OPTIMAL} on \emph{both} sides:
they are execution and memory dependencies, not layout transitions. \blade{}
emits no image barrier at all. So neither implementation performs a
steady-state layout transition on these workloads, and the layout difference
between them is static --- which layout the driver sees the image in --- rather
than a per-pass cost. Any layout effect in the end-to-end numbers is therefore
a property of \texttt{GENERAL} versus \texttt{COLOR\_ATTACHMENT\_OPTIMAL} as
states, not of moving between them.

\subsubsection*{The same commands on every driver}

Repeating the captures on two RADV machines and on Intel's driver settles what
varies and what does not. Normalizing each capture to a table of one row per
barrier --- implementation, workload, policy, stage and access masks, barrier
kind, layouts --- all \bladenum{captures/machines} machines produce
\bladenum{captures/rows} rows each and the tables are identical. Not similar:
the same SHA-256, \texttt{\bladenum{captures/sha}}, on
\bladenum{captures/machines} machines running three drivers.

That is worth stating plainly because it removes an entire class of
explanation. Every vendor difference in Sections~\ref{sec:placement}
and~\ref{sec:scope-results} is a difference in how a driver and a GPU
\emph{respond} to commands, not a difference in the commands they were given.
When narrowing the barrier scope is worth
$\bmag{rtx5070}{graphics-chain}{scope}$ on the RTX~5070,
$\bmag{intelxe}{graphics-chain}{scope}$ on the Intel part, and nothing on any
AMD device, the submitted stream in all four cases is the same stream.

It also puts the failed prediction of Section~\ref{sec:scope-results} on a
firmer footing. That section argues from RADV's source that a narrowed scope
sheds specific cache and drain operations, and finds no time saved. The
captures confirm the input to that argument was right --- the commands do change
exactly as described, on RADV as everywhere else --- so what failed was the
inference from commands to cost, not the reading of the driver. A command
stream bounds what the driver is asked to do. Only a measurement says what the
asking costs.

One case is deliberately not covered. Every workload here is single-sampled,
and Section~\ref{sec:radv-barriers} reads from RADV that \texttt{GENERAL}
disables FMASK compression unconditionally, so multisampled colour is the one
configuration where a persistent \texttt{GENERAL} layout should cost real
bandwidth on AMD. We state that from the driver source and do not test it. The
honest summary is that this paper establishes the layout question for
single-sampled targets and leaves the multisampled case where it found it: a
documented exception in a driver, not a measurement.

The one layout claim this draft does make is negative and comes from the
sources rather than the measurements: on RADV, a persistent \texttt{GENERAL}
layout is free for DCC on RDNA and later, conditionally free for depth, and
never free for multisampled colour. Our workloads use single-sampled
\texttt{RGBA8} targets, so they sit on the favourable side of all three
predicates; a study that wanted to stress the layout dimension would have to
add MSAA. The \texttt{matrix} machine is the only one whose driver does not
advertise \texttt{VK\_KHR\_unified\_image\_layouts}, and \blade{} still
outperforms \wgpu{} there on both graphics workloads. That is
consistent with the missing extension not dominating, but it is an end-to-end
observation and not a layout experiment.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The results support a decision boundary rather than a winner.

\textbf{Placement dominates precision, in both directions.} The largest device
effect we measured comes from barrier \emph{placement} within one
implementation, not from tracking. The best configuration on the RTX~5070 for
independent render passes is the one with no tracker and no barriers
(\bus{rtx5070}{graphics-independent}{placement}), followed by the tracked
implementation (\bus{rtx5070}{graphics-independent}{wgpu}), followed by the
untracked implementation with redundant barriers
(\bus{rtx5070}{graphics-independent}{auto}). An abstraction layer that tracks
resources precisely but still cannot express ``these passes are unrelated'' is
leaving most of the benefit on the table.

\textbf{The device decides, and reading the driver does not substitute for
asking it.} The captures show that all \bladenum{captures/machines} machines
submit an identical command stream (Section~\ref{sec:generated}), so every
vendor difference in this paper is a difference in response rather than in
request. Against that fixed input, two predictions we made from mechanism were
wrong in the same direction. Redundant barriers between independent render
passes cost $\bmag{rtx5070}{graphics-independent}{placement}$ on NVIDIA and
nothing on either desktop AMD device, despite AMD being the architecture whose
vendor guidance warns hardest about broad barriers. Narrowing the barrier scope
removes commands from the RADV stream that it does not remove from the NVIDIA
or Intel one, and pays on NVIDIA and Intel and not on AMD. Both times the
source told us what would be emitted and we took that for what it would cost.

The second failure had an answer in the same file, and finding it afterwards is
the part worth keeping. \texttt{radv\_dst\_access\_flush} discards the
destination-side cache invalidation our narrower barrier was designed to avoid,
on every part we measured, without being asked
(Section~\ref{sec:radv-elision}). We had read the function that translates a
request into work and not the one that decides how much of that work to do.
Counting emitted commands bounds how much the driver is \emph{asked} for; the
bound can be arbitrarily loose, because a drain is free when there is nothing
in flight to drain and an invalidation is free when the driver has already
proved it unnecessary. Mechanism is good for explaining a measurement and poor
for replacing one --- and a mechanism that only explains what has already been
measured is still worth having, because it says where the result should
generalize. Here it says: on a part whose TCC is not coherent with the render
backends, the narrow barrier should pay on AMD too. We did not have one to try.

\textbf{The workload envelope for coarse barriers.} Tracking-free
synchronization with pass-boundary barriers is appropriate when passes form
chains, when the device is already saturated by each pass, or when host cost
dominates. It is inappropriate when independent passes have complementary
bottlenecks and spare device capacity, which is where the field report sits. An
encoder-level opt-out covers that case at negligible API cost, which is why it
was the right resolution to the field report~\cite{blade-issue-343}: it turns
the expensive case into a two-line change without adding resource states to the
API.

\textbf{The host-side case is the strong one.} It is consistent across four
vendors, large in relative terms
($\bladenum{host/ratio/min}$--$\bladenum{host/ratio/max}\times$), and small in
absolute terms at low pass counts. A renderer submitting a few hundred passes
per frame saves on the order of a millisecond of CPU time per frame; one
submitting a dozen saves tens of microseconds. The design is most valuable to
applications that are CPU-bound on command recording, and this is exactly the
population that also tends to know its own dependency graph.

\textbf{The negative results matter.} The Radeon~780M shows that removing
redundant barriers is not a free optimization, and the AMD parts show that
a saving visible in a command stream need not be visible in a timer. A layer
that exposes manual barriers must therefore treat them as a measured
optimization rather than a default, and profiling guidance belongs next to the
API.

\subsection{What to do on AMD}
\label{sec:amd-advice}

The short answer to ``does this abstraction work on AMD'' is that its host-side
advantage holds there as firmly as anywhere ---
$\bladenum{rx7900xt/compute-independent/hostwgpuus}\,\mu$s against
$\bladenum{rx7900xt/compute-independent/hostautous}\,\mu$s for the same sixteen
passes on the RX~7900~XT --- and its device-side cost is the widest-ranging of
any vendor in the study, from
$\bmag{rx7900xt}{compute-independent}{placement}$ recoverable to
$\bmag{radeon780m}{graphics-independent}{placement}$ lost by trying. Three
things follow, in the order an application should try them.

\emph{Do not expect the narrow barrier to help here.} It is tempting to read
Section~\ref{sec:radv-barriers} as a shopping list of savings, and we did.
Narrowing the scope removes real commands from the RADV stream, and it buys
$\bmag{rtx5070}{graphics-chain}{scope}$ on NVIDIA and
$\bmag{intelxe}{graphics-chain}{scope}$ on Intel, but on AMD it bought nothing
we could measure in any cell precise enough to say
(Section~\ref{sec:scope-results}), and Section~\ref{sec:radv-elision} says why:
RADV had already discarded the destination half of it. It costs nothing either,
and it is the one change an application cannot get wrong, so there is no reason
to avoid it --- but it is not the AMD fix.

\emph{Restructure the passes so the remaining barriers are ones you need.}
This is the change that pays. On the RX~7900~XT, sixteen independent compute
passes run $\bmagci{rx7900xt}{compute-independent}{placement}$ faster once the
redundant barriers are gone, and the mixed equivalent
$\bmag{rx7900xt}{mixed-independent}{placement}$ faster. The field report's
renderer serialized because independent work sat on either side of a barrier.
The fix is not only to delete that barrier but to group the independent passes
so one barrier separates each group from the next --- ray tracing with the
reflection prepass, both view passes together, then the resolves, then the
dependent refraction pass. With \texttt{manual\_barriers} this is a change to
where \texttt{barrier()} is called, not a change to any resource declaration,
and the encoder-level flag makes the scope of the change explicit. Applications
that want the automatic policy for most of a frame can put the restructured
part in its own encoder and submit both to the same queue.

\emph{Then measure, because the restructuring is not always a win.} On the
Radeon~780M, letting sixteen independent render passes overlap cost
$\bmag{radeon780m}{graphics-independent}{placement}$ and the mixed equivalent
$\bmag{radeon780m}{mixed-independent}{placement}$, while on the RX~7900~XT the
same change on render passes bought nothing at all and the compute equivalent
bought a quarter of the frame. Two AMD parts of the same generation therefore
disagree about the sign, which is the strongest reason in this paper not to
ship the change unmeasured. The passes worth grouping are the ones with
complementary bottlenecks --- ray tracing next to rasterization, a depth
prepass next to a compute simulation --- not several instances of the same
bottleneck. A GPU profiler showing the two passes actually overlapping, as in
the field report, is the evidence to look for; a frame-time delta on its own
will not distinguish a scheduling win from whatever the 780M is doing.

Note also that suppressing the encoder's barriers forfeits the scope narrowing
on the boundaries that remain, since an explicitly placed barrier describes
only its source. On AMD that costs nothing today, because the narrowing buys
nothing there; on NVIDIA it is a real trade.

Two things we looked for and did not find are worth recording, because they
bound what an implementation can do without becoming a tracker. There is no
cheaper barrier to emit: the narrowed scope already removes everything
Section~\ref{sec:radv-barriers} identifies as removable without naming a
resource, and it changes nothing on AMD. And the pessimism that a global
barrier does cause in RADV --- the unconditional metadata flushes, the
unusable coherence check --- is only avoidable by supplying an image, which is
the state \blade{} declines to keep. The remaining AMD lever is placement, and
placement is the application's knowledge, not the layer's.

The one case where AMD still argues for genuine layout tracking is
multisampled colour, where RADV disables FMASK compression in
\texttt{GENERAL}. That is orthogonal to everything above and is not addressed
by either barrier axis.

\section{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:threats}

GPU timestamps perturb the measurement: the same host cost is larger with them
enabled, by a margin that grows with the pass count. We therefore separate
CPU-only and GPU-timed collections and take host claims only from the former. Absolute host numbers in Table~\ref{tab:host-matrix} carry
instrumentation overhead and are used for direction and order of magnitude.

Driver heuristics may recognize repeated synthetic work; the benchmark varies a
per-iteration seed while preserving the graph shape, but does not vary resource
identity. Results from one driver version cannot establish permanent vendor
behaviour, and all raw metadata is retained so a rerun can be compared.

The controlled \blade{} variants now isolate two of the four factors ---
placement and stage/access scope --- but still not global versus
resource-specific memory scope, nor persistent \texttt{GENERAL} versus
usage-specific layouts. Those two are argued from driver sources and observed
end-to-end through \wgpu, so causal claims about them are limited by
construction. The scoped results cover five Vulkan device-driver pairs across
three vendors, but not every cell within them:
\bladenum{floor/above2} of \bladenum{floor/count} have control floors above
$2\%$, and every conclusion is checked against the floor of the cell it comes
from. Synchronization validation passes on every workload and policy, which
rules out the obvious way a narrowed scope could be wrong, but it is evidence
about the schedules that were executed rather than a proof.
End-to-end \wgpu{} differences are labelled as such throughout, and the ones we
cannot account for --- the Intel gap in one direction, the RX~7900~XT gap in
the other --- are left unexplained rather than attributed.

The workloads are microbenchmarks of sixteen passes each. Two of the six
alternate compute and render work, so heterogeneity is represented, but only
the synthetic kind: two pass types in a fixed alternation, with identical work
in every instance of each. Real frames vary the cost, the resources, and the
bottleneck from pass to pass, which is precisely the regime in which the field
report saw a benefit and our \texttt{graphics-independent} workload saw a loss
on one device. The boundary proposed in Section~\ref{sec:discussion} is
therefore a hypothesis supported by microbenchmarks --- including mixed ones ---
and one field report, not a validated rule. We chose not to close that gap with
application workloads: doing so would trade a controlled comparison for a
plausible one, since scenes, content, and driver heuristics vary in ways this
design exists to hold still. A reader who needs the rule validated on their own
frame should measure their own frame, which is the recommendation
Section~\ref{sec:amd-advice} makes anyway.

The AMD evidence is now a discrete RDNA3 part, a mobile RDNA3 iGPU, a very
small desktop iGPU, the driver sources, and a user report. The small iGPU is
the most precisely measured device in the study and the least interesting, since
it is saturated by a single pass; the discrete part carries the largest AMD
claims and has the widest intervals behind them, at floors of
$\bfloor{rx7900xt}{compute-independent}$ and
$\bfloor{rx7900xt}{mixed-independent}$ for the two cells where placement pays.
Those two should be repeated with enough warm-up for the device to stop
accelerating before being leaned on --- its clocks were already pinned as far
as the driver allows, and that was not enough --- and the third AMD part
disagrees with them in sign on the render workloads, which is itself part of
the result rather than a contradiction of it.

\section{Related Work}

The Vulkan specification defines explicit execution and memory dependencies
and application-controlled image layouts~\cite{vulkan-spec}. AMD and NVIDIA
have published architecture-specific synchronization guidance
~\cite{amd-barriers,amd-rdna-guide,nvidia-vulkan-tips}. The
unified-image-layouts extension standardizes the observation that specialized
layouts no longer correspond to distinct representations on many modern
implementations~\cite{vulkan-unified-layouts}; the RADV predicates quoted in
this paper are a concrete instance of that convergence, including its
exceptions.

WebGPU specifies portable usage-scope validation~\cite{webgpu-spec}; \wgpu{}
implements it using optimized dense resource trackers~\cite{wgpu-trackers}.
Metal takes a third position, performing hazard tracking in the framework with
an opt-out per resource~\cite{metal-hazard-tracking}. \blade{} occupies a
fourth point by exposing an unsafe native contract and relying on
application-declared pass dependencies.

\section{Conclusion}

Fine-grained resource states do not pay for themselves in most of the cases we
measured, but coarse synchronization does not come free either, and the two
costs live on different sides of the interface. Tracking costs host time
consistently ---
$\bladenum{host/ratio/min}$--$\bladenum{host/ratio/max}\times$ across four
vendors --- and buys back device time only where it can prove independence,
which it did on some devices and not others. Placing a barrier at every pass
boundary costs nothing when passes are dependent, costs up to
$\bmag{rtx5070}{graphics-independent}{placement}$ of device time when they are
not and the device has spare capacity, and \emph{saves}
$\bmag{radeon780m}{graphics-independent}{placement}$ on one integrated part for
reasons we could not establish. Which of those three a given application is in
is not predictable from the vendor, the API, or the driver source; it has to be
measured.

The practical conclusion is that the expressive gap that matters is not
per-resource state but the ability to say ``these passes are unrelated''. An
encoder-level opt-out gives an application that already knows its dependency
graph the whole benefit of tracking and more, at a fraction of the host cost
and none of the API surface. It should be presented as a profiled optimization
rather than a default, because on at least one real device it makes things
worse.

What this draft does not have, and has decided not to pursue, is stated rather
than promised: application frames, multisampled targets, and repeats --- with
enough warm-up to reach a steady clock --- of the cells whose control floor
exceeds the effect in them. Each is a bound on a specific claim, named where
that claim is made.
